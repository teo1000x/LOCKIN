# 21 - Name and Logo

> Supersedes `docs/07-NAMING.md` and `docs/07-NAMING-V2.md`. Both are kept for the record.
> Every name locked or ranked in those two documents is rejected here. Reasons are in Part 0.

Product: software that builds and runs Facebook and Instagram ad campaigns for local service
businesses. The owner connects their own Meta ad account. The software writes the marketing
plan, generates the ads, and publishes only the ads the owner has individually approved, one at
a time. 96 trades across 17 categories. Pricing $499, $1,300 and $2,500 per month. Working
placeholder name: Foreman.

**Bonded Crew**, on `bondedcrew.com`. Verified unregistered at the Verisign registry. No

Runner up: **Crew Chief Works**, on `crewchiefworks.com`.

## Part 0: why the first two passes failed, and the rule applied here

Three separate failure modes have now been recorded on this project. All three are the same
mistake wearing different clothes: a name that looked right and was never checked against how
the word is actually used by somebody else.

1. **A trade word that means money lost.** V1 locked a name built on a root that, in the trades,
   means an unbillable return trip to fix work that was done wrong. The name told an HVAC owner
   we sell software about his rework rate. Banned outright, including in compounds. So is any
   other word meaning rework, defect, or complaint.
2. **A single-trade word.** Shingle and eave are roofing vocabulary. With 96 trades in scope, a
   roofing word excludes 95 percent of the buyers.
3. **A weak coinage.** V2 locked Permitmark. The root was researched and clean, but the name was
   a bolt-on compound that describes nothing and pictures nothing. Judged weak and generic.

There is a fourth failure mode that V2 discovered and this pass hit again, harder: **words that
denote marketing or approval are already owned by marketing and approval companies.** V2 lost
Frontage, Marquee, Spotlight, Placard and Manifest this way. This pass lost four more families
the same way. Details in Part 2.

**The rule applied throughout this pass.** A root had to clear four gates before it could be
built into a candidate:

- Does a tradesperson use this word for something going wrong, or something they fix for free?
- Is the word specific to one trade, or does it work for a gym, a dog groomer, and a roofer?
- Does a real, currently operating marketing, advertising, or field service software company
  already use this root?
- Can a 55 year old roofer say it on the phone and spell it after hearing it once?

Roughly 420 candidate domains were checked at the registry across ten batches. Most died at the
domain gate. The survivors then went through collision search, which killed four whole families.

## Part 1: eight candidates

Every name below has a verified unregistered `.com`. Evidence is in Part 2. They are ranked.
Numbers 7 and 8 are included because they were strong on the page and are recorded here as
found and rejected, so a future pass does not rediscover them and repeat the mistake.

### 1. Bonded Crew

`bondedcrew.com`

**What it means.** "Licensed, bonded and insured" is the trust phrase every one of the 96 trades
puts in its own advertising. A bond means somebody vetted the business and put money behind it.
Cleaners, groomers, detailers, tree services and gyms all carry bonds, so this is not
construction vocabulary. Crew is the plainest word in the trades for the people who do the work.

**Why it fits this product.** This software connects to a stranger's Meta ad account and can
spend money on their card. The single hardest thing it has to sell is trust. Bonded Crew says
what the product has to earn: we are the crew you can hand the keys to. It also maps onto the
inviolable rules without straining. Nothing publishes without the owner's approval, our access
is scoped, we never touch the media spend.

**Tone.** Trustworthy, plain, slightly formal, adult. Sounds like a firm that has been around,
not a launch.

**Risk.** "Bonded" also means glued in a materials context, and a bonded warehouse is a customs
term. Neither is negative and neither is trade-facing. Secondary risk: on first hearing, a
listener may briefly place it as an insurance or surety product rather than software. That
resolves on the first sentence of explanation.

### 2. Crew Chief Works

`crewchiefworks.com`

**What it means.** A crew chief runs the job so the owner does not have to stand there. It is
the same idea as the Foreman placeholder, in a phrase that is universal instead of
construction-flavoured, and it carries a warm second association with race teams for exactly
this buyer. "Works" is the trade shop suffix, as in ironworks and waterworks.

**Why it fits this product.** The product is a competent second in command for marketing. This
is the most direct restatement of the Foreman idea available with a clean domain.

**Tone.** Capable, in charge, blue collar, unpretentious.

**Risk.** `crewchief.com` belongs to somebody else, registered 1997, so the brand and the domain
are not identical. That is the exact shape of the Foreman problem, where `foremanhq.com` was
already taken. "Works" is also filler and adds a syllable. Crewchief Systems exists in aviation
asset software, a different category but a name that will appear in search.

### 3. All Clear Crew

`allclearcrew.com`

**What it means.** "All clear" is universal, positive, and understood by every trade and every
adult. It means checked and safe to go.

**Why it fits this product.** It is the approval gate stated in plain English. Nothing runs until
the owner gives the all clear.

**Tone.** Reassuring, safety-first, plain.

**Risk.** Two real problems. First, "All Clear" is a common name for local cleaning companies,
and cleaning is one of our 96 trades, so a cleaning-company buyer searching for us will land on
their own competitors. Second, the phrase is a mild tongue twister. The "clear crew" consonant
run is awkward to say quickly on a phone, which is a genuine defect for a name whose main test
is a phone call.

### 4. All Clear Board

`allclearboard.com`

**What it means.** The board of ads waiting for the owner's all clear, which is a real screen in
the product.

**Why it fits this product.** It names the actual review surface rather than an abstraction.

**Tone.** Functional, workmanlike.

**Risk.** Same "All Clear" search noise as number 3. "Board" is also slightly cool and clerical
next to "Crew".

### 5. Estimate Crew

`estimatecrew.com`

**What it means.** An estimate is the one document common to every job in every one of the 96
trades. V2 researched this root directly and found it neutral to positive with no negative trade
usage.

**Why it fits this product.** Weakly. It ties to winning work, which is what the ads are for.

**Tone.** Practical, plain.

**Risk.** The bigger problem is category confusion. "Estimate" tells a contractor this is
quoting software, which is a crowded and completely different category. EstimateRocket and
EstiMate Software both exist. The name would spend its life explaining what it is not.

### 6. Muster House

`musterhouse.com`

**What it means.** To muster is to gather the crew and call the roll. A muster house is where
that happens.

**Why it fits this product.** The product gathers the campaign, lines up the ads, and presents
them for the owner to go down the list.

**Tone.** Old trade, orderly, a little military.

**Risk.** "Muster" is the least common word in this list. Some buyers will know it only from the
phrase "pass muster". It is spellable but it is not everyday speech for a 55 year old roofer,
which is the stated bar. Ranked here because it is clean, not because it is strong.

### 7. Signoff Board

`signoffboard.com`. **Domain is free. Name is rejected.**

A sign-off is universal and positive, and the name states the inviolable rule exactly. It was
the leading candidate for most of this pass. It is rejected on a direct category collision:
SignOff is a currently listed product selling branded digital work order approval software to
contractors and service professionals. That is our buyer, and an adjacent product category.
There is a second operator, ClearSignoff, in workflow approval management. Details in Part 2.

### 8. Hometown Board

`hometownboard.com`. **Domain is free. Name is rejected.**

Warm, local, universal across all 96 trades, and completely free of trade-negative meaning.
Rejected on collision. "Hometown" is heavily occupied by local-business marketing agencies,
including at least one that sells Google and Meta ads to small businesses, which is our exact
category. Details in Part 2.

## Part 2: domain verification and collision findings

### 2a. Method, and a correction to the method used in V1 and V2

**Do not use `dig`.** DNS resolution says nothing about registration. A registered, parked domain
returns no A record and reads as free. V1 and V2 both used `dig` as the first pass and both
nearly shipped a taken domain because of it.

**`whois name.com | head -20` is also unreliable on this machine.** The system `whois` client
queries IANA first, and the first 20 or more lines are Verisign registry boilerplate that is
byte-identical whether the name is free or taken. Here is the actual output for a domain that is
free, showing that the first 20 lines contain no verdict at all:

```
$ whois bondedcrew.com | head -20
% IANA WHOIS server
% for more information on IANA, visit http://www.iana.org
% This query returned 1 object

refer:        whois.verisign-grs.com

domain:       COM

organisation: VeriSign Global Registry Services
address:      12061 Bluemont Way
address:      Reston VA 20190
address:      United States of America (the)
...
```

**The method used here.** Query the `.com` registry directly and read its verdict line:

```
whois -h whois.verisign-grs.com <name>.com
```

A domain is available only if that returns `No match for domain`. Anything returning a
`Creation Date:` is registered, regardless of whether a website loads.

## Part 3: logo direction for the top three

The palette is fixed. Cobalt `#2454D9` on the near-white canvas `#F6F7F9`, with the cool grey
ramp for text. There is one accent and there is never a second. Type is Public Sans for
everything and IBM Plex Mono for numerals only, so **no wordmark below is set in Plex Mono**, and
no new typeface is introduced. If a display face is ever wanted for the wordmark it has to come
back as an amendment to `docs/08-DESIGN-SYSTEM.md`, not as a logo decision.

Two sizes govern every choice below. At 24px in a browser tab you get about a 5 by 5 grid of
distinguishable features, so the mark carries at most two or three elements. At 40px in the app
rail you can afford one more. Both specs assume a 24 by 24 box with a 2px clear margin, giving a
20 by 20 artwork field, scaled proportionally for 40px.

Solid cobalt fills are preferred over stroke-only marks at tab size, because a hairline stroke
disappears against a light browser chrome. Where a stroke is used it is 2.0 weight normalised,
matching the locked global icon stroke width.

### 3.1 Bonded Crew

**Wordmark.** "Bonded Crew" in Public Sans ExtraBold, weight 800, sentence case, one line, single
word space, tracking `-0.02em`. Colour: the full wordmark in grey-900 `#262B36`. The accent does
not appear in the wordmark, because the mark carries it and the system allows only one accent.
Cap height sets the unit. The mark sits to the left of the wordmark with a gap of half the cap
height, and the mark's optical centre aligns to the wordmark's optical centre, not to its
baseline. There is no tagline in the lockup.

Why Public Sans 800 and not the 900 Black weight: at 800 the counters in the letters o, e and d
stay open at small sizes, which is the whole reason the typeface was chosen for this audience.
900 closes them up on a phone.

**Mark: the stamped tag.**

- One square, 20 by 20, corner radius 3.3px, which is one sixth of the side, so it scales to the
  locked `radius-md` proportion at 40px and reads as `radius-sm` at 16px. Filled solid cobalt
  `#2454D9`. No border, no shadow, no gradient, no glow.
- Two knocked-out bars in the square, both in the page canvas colour `#F6F7F9` so the mark works
  on white and on the off-white canvas alike. Both bars have fully rounded ends, radius equal to
  half their height.
- Upper bar: 7px wide, 3px tall, left edge at x equals 4.5, top edge at y equals 6.5.
- Lower bar: 11px wide, 3px tall, left edge at x equals 4.5, top edge at y equals 12.
- The two bars are deliberately unequal in length and left-aligned. Three equal bars would read
  as a hamburger menu icon. Two unequal, left-aligned bars read as lines of text on a tag or a
  plate.

**Why it suits this buyer.** It is a stamped plate. A licence plate, a bonding certificate, a
permit tag, an inspector's stamp on work that passed. That object is familiar and positive in
every one of the 96 trades, and it is the visual form of the product's one inviolable rule:
nothing goes out without an approval on it. It avoids the checkmark, which is the single most
overused mark in software.

**At 24px** it reads as a solid cobalt tile with two light bars. Distinct in a tab strip because
almost nothing else there is a filled saturated square. **At 16px**, drop the upper bar and keep
the lower one, so the mark stays a tile with one clear notch of light. **At 40px in the app rail**,
mark only, no wordmark, artwork at 24px optical size centred in the 40px square with 8px padding.

### 3.2 Crew Chief Works

**Wordmark.** "Crew Chief Works" is three words and too long for a rail or a narrow header, so the
lockup has two forms. The primary wordmark sets "Crew Chief" in Public Sans ExtraBold 800,
sentence case, tracking `-0.02em`, grey-900 `#262B36`, with "Works" following in Public Sans
Medium 500 at the same optical size in grey-600 `#5C6884`. The weight and colour drop makes the
suffix recede, so the brand people say out loud is "Crew Chief" while the legal and domain name
stays intact. The secondary wordmark drops "Works" entirely and is used anywhere under 200px wide.

**Mark: the rank chevron.**

- One chevron, drawn as a stroked path, not a filled triangle. Stroke 3.5px at 24px, mitred
  joins, flat caps, cobalt `#2454D9`.
- Apex at x 10, y 6.5. Left arm runs down to x 2, y 14.5. Right arm runs down to x 18, y 14.5.
  The apex is offset one unit left of centre so the mark has a direction and does not sit as a
  symmetrical decoration.
- A second chevron below, identical angle and stroke, apex at x 10, y 13, arms to x 4.5, y 18.5
  and x 15.5, y 18.5. Same cobalt at full strength. Do not tint the second chevron, since tints of
  the accent are reserved for `accent-subtle` backgrounds in the system.
- Two chevrons is the ceiling. Three is illegible at tab size.

**Why it suits this buyer.** Chevrons are rank. Anyone who has worked on a crew, served, or
watched a race team reads two stacked chevrons as "the one in charge" without being told. That is
the exact promise of the product: a competent second in command who runs the job so the owner
does not have to. It is also the only mark of the three that carries motion, pointing up and
forward, which suits a name about running the work.

**At 24px** two stroked chevrons hold, because the stroke is heavy relative to the field.
**At 16px**, drop to a single chevron scaled to fill the field. **At 40px in the app rail**, the
two-chevron mark centred with 8px padding, no wordmark.

The one risk to manage in execution: a chevron pair is a common generic mark. Keep it saved by the
off-centre apex and the heavy stroke. Do not round the joins, do not add a circle around it, and
do not let it become a symmetrical arrow.

### 3.3 All Clear Crew

**Wordmark.** "All Clear Crew" in Public Sans Bold, weight 700, sentence case, tracking `-0.01em`,
grey-900 `#262B36`. This name uses 700 rather than 800 because it is three short words and the
heavier weight makes the two "Cl" and "Cr" clusters crowd. Set on one line. Word spacing at
default, not tightened, so the three words stay separable at small sizes. Mark to the left, gap of
half the cap height, optical centres aligned.

**Mark: the raised gate arm.**

- One vertical post: a rectangle 3.5px wide by 20px tall, corner radius 1.75px, filled cobalt
  `#2454D9`, left edge at x equals 2.
- One gate arm: a rectangle 16px long by 3.5px tall, corner radius 1.75px, filled cobalt, rotated
  38 degrees anticlockwise about its own left end, with that left end anchored to the post at x
  equals 5.5, y equals 12.
- Nothing else. Two elements only. No second post, no ground line, no arrow.

**Why it suits this buyer.** A barrier arm in the raised position means cleared, go ahead. Every
trade crosses a gate into a yard, a lot, a site, or a gated neighbourhood, and every one of them
reads a raised arm the same way. It is the plainest possible picture of the product's rule: the
owner lifts the gate, one ad at a time, and nothing passes until they do.

**At 24px** it reads as a bold cobalt L rotated open, which is unusual enough in a tab strip to be
findable. **At 16px**, increase the arm's rotation to 45 degrees and shorten it to 12px so the
angle stays legible. **At 40px in the app rail**, mark only, 24px optical size, 8px padding.

## Final recommendation

**Lock Bonded Crew. Buy `bondedcrew.com` now.**

Five reasons, in order of weight.

1. **It solves the product's hardest problem, which is trust.** This software asks a stranger to
   connect a Meta ad account and let generated content spend money on their own card. Every other
   feature is downstream of whether they believe us. "Licensed, bonded and insured" is the exact
   phrase this buyer already uses to make that argument to their own customers. The name borrows
   their own trust vocabulary and points it at us.
2. **The root is verified clean on all four gates.** It is not rework vocabulary. It is not
   single-trade vocabulary, and it genuinely covers cleaners, groomers, detailers and gyms as well
   as roofers. No marketing or advertising company was found using it. It is two everyday words.
3. **The name and the domain are the same thing.** No suffix, no HQ, no abbreviation, no
   compromise. That was the specific defect that ended Foreman.
4. **It pictures something.** Permitmark was rejected as weak because it described nothing and
   showed nothing. Bonded Crew has an object behind it, which is why the logo direction in Part
   3.1 writes itself as a stamped plate rather than an abstract shape.
5. **It passes the phone test.** Two words, six syllables of common English, no consonant
   pile-up, unambiguous spelling. "I use Bonded Crew for my ads" is a sentence a 55 year old
   roofer says without thinking about it.

**Runner up: Crew Chief Works, on `crewchiefworks.com`.**

It is the better metaphor. It is the Foreman idea restated in words that work for all 96 trades,
and it is the most likeable name in the set. It loses on two mechanical points rather than on
taste. `crewchief.com` belongs to somebody else, which reproduces the exact domain mismatch that
killed Foreman, and "Works" is a syllable of filler that the brand will spend years dropping in
conversation and carrying in writing. Take it if the founder wants warmth over gravity and is
willing to live with a domain that is not the brand.

**Third: All Clear Crew, on `allclearcrew.com`.** Clean domain, clean meaning, and the best
statement of the approval gate in the set. Held at third because it shares a name with local
cleaning companies who are inside our own buyer base, and because the phrase is genuinely awkward
to say at speed, which is the one test this name has to pass.

**Before purchase, re-run the registry check.** Three domains in this pass were registered in the
weeks before it, and one two days before. The verdict in Part 2b is true as of today and not a
day longer.
